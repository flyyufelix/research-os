# Eysenck's Theory of Personality and the Role of Background Music

## Citation

Küssner, M. B. (2017). *Eysenck's theory of personality and the role of background music in cognitive task performance: A mini-review of conflicting findings and a new perspective*. Frontiers in Psychology, 8, 1991. [https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01991](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01991)

Local copy: [source.pdf](source.pdf). The publisher labels the article CC BY.

## Material examined and reading-depth rationale

Reading depth: **full**. The complete six-page article was read, including its review argument, evidence table, EEG discussion, proposed research direction, and stated qualifications. Full reading was appropriate because the paper directly challenges a candidate explanation—extraversion—and helps define what evidence the project should seek next.

## Source claims

The author argues that research has found beneficial, detrimental, and null relationships between background music and cognitive performance. Studies testing Eysenck's arousal-based prediction provide roughly as much evidence against it as for it, so questionnaire-measured extraversion alone does not yield a clear account.

The paper further argues that EEG alpha power may be an inadequate stand-alone marker of cortical arousal. It proposes investigating beta power as a possible predictor of cognitive performance and testing whether focused music listening *before* a task could be more useful than music played during it. The latter is explicitly a hypothesis, not an established intervention.

## Method and evidence

This is a narrative mini-review rather than a systematic review or meta-analysis. It surveys decades of background-music studies, then tabulates studies that compared introverts and extraverts under music, noise, or silence. The table covers multiple tasks—including reading, memory, reasoning, arithmetic, and Stroop tests—and displays mixed support across studies and sometimes across tasks within the same study.

The article also reviews conflicting associations among extraversion, alpha-band activity, and beta-band activity. It uses that literature to motivate a new research program rather than to estimate an effect size.

## Main findings

- The background-music literature contains positive, negative, and null findings across different cognitive tasks.
- Evidence for the predicted interaction between extraversion and background stimulation is inconsistent.
- Many supportive studies show harm to introverts rather than a benefit to extraverts.
- Alpha power does not provide a consistently supported bridge between extraversion and cortical arousal.
- Beta power and focused pre-task listening are proposed as testable directions, but the causal chain remains unestablished.

## Limitations

- The review does not report a systematic search, formal quality assessment, or quantitative synthesis.
- Included tasks, music, samples, and measures are highly heterogeneous.
- The physiological discussion relies on a small and conflicting evidence base.
- The focused-listening proposal is speculative and should not be presented as study advice.

## Project relevance

The review reinforces the project's preference for conditional explanations. It prevents the manifesto from treating extraversion as a settled moderator and suggests distinguishing background stimulation during a task from music used before a task to regulate mood or arousal.

## Tensions and connections

The review's broad mixed picture contrasts productively with the narrow experimental result in [Sun et al. (2024)](../sun-2024-background-music-reading-comprehension/note.md). Together they illustrate why precise definitions of the task, music, outcome, and participant characteristics matter.

## Questions to carry forward

- Which moderators replicate when tasks, music, and outcomes are tightly controlled?
- Does extraversion add predictive value beyond listening habit, working memory, preference, and baseline performance?
- Can pre-task listening improve later performance, and is beta power a mediator or merely a correlate?
