# Impact of Background Music on Reading Comprehension

## Citation

Sun, Y., Sun, C., Li, C., Shao, X., Liu, Q., & Liu, H. (2024). *Impact of background music on reading comprehension: influence of lyrics language and study habits*. Frontiers in Psychology, 15, 1363562. [https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1363562](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1363562)

Local copy: [source.pdf](source.pdf). The publisher labels the article CC BY.

## Material examined and reading-depth rationale

Reading depth: **full**. The complete ten-page article was read, including participant selection, design, materials, procedure, statistical analysis, tables, figures, discussion, limitations, and data statement. Full reading was warranted because the study directly tests the project's central lyrics and listening-habit hypotheses.

## Source claims

The authors conclude that lyrical pop music reduced reading-comprehension accuracy relative to no music, that disruption was greater when lyrics and text used the same language, and that habitual music listeners were less disrupted than non-listeners. They interpret the result through a duplex-mechanism account of auditory distraction.

## Method and evidence

The study recruited 90 students aged 18–21 from Shandong Sport University in China. A researcher-designed 15-item questionnaire selected two deliberately separated habit groups: 45 listeners scoring above 60 and 45 non-listeners scoring below 30. Within each habit group, participants were randomly assigned to Mandarin pop music, English pop music, or no music, yielding 15 participants per cell.

All participants completed both Chinese- and English-language reading tasks. Music condition and listening habit were between-participant factors; text language was within-participant. The two familiar pop songs were presented through headphones at 60–65 dB(A). Comprehension accuracy came from true/false literal and inferential questions after six short passages. The authors analyzed accuracy using a three-way mixed ANOVA with Bonferroni-corrected follow-ups.

Baseline reading performance did not significantly differ across the three music-condition groups. The paper reports large main effects for listening habit and music type, plus large listening-habit × music-type and music-type × text-language interactions. The three-way interaction was not significant.

## Main findings

- Both lyrical-music conditions generally produced lower comprehension accuracy than no music.
- Same-language interference appeared in both directions: Mandarin lyrics were especially disruptive for Chinese texts, and English lyrics for English texts.
- Non-listeners performed far worse under lyrical music than habitual listeners.
- Among listeners, music still showed some disadvantage relative to no music; habit reduced disruption but did not establish a learning benefit.

## Limitations

- Each experimental cell contained only 15 participants, all from one specialist university.
- The habit groups were selected from extreme questionnaire scores, so the result does not describe the full continuum of students.
- Listening habit was observed, not assigned; adaptation cannot be separated from self-selection or other individual differences.
- Only two familiar lyrical pop songs were used. Instrumental music, unfamiliar music, genre, preference, and emotional response were not isolated.
- English proficiency, cognitive control, and working memory were not assessed.
- Music condition was between participants; a within-person comparison could control more individual variation.
- The behavioral design cannot establish the proposed neural mechanism. Raw data are available only from the authors upon reasonable request.

## Project relevance

This is direct but narrow evidence that intelligible lyrics can compete with reading, especially when lyric and text languages match. It also sharpens the project distinction between *being less disrupted* and *benefiting*: habitual listeners' relative resilience does not show that music improves their comprehension.

## Tensions and connections

The study's strong conditional effects coexist with the broader mixed evidence reviewed by [Küssner (2017)](../kussner-2017-background-music-personality/note.md), which cautions against generalizing from one task, music condition, or individual-difference measure.

The [study-session journal](../../../notes/study-session-journal.md) independently raises a different outcome: lyrical music may make work easier to begin even if comprehension suffers. Sun et al. did not measure that trade-off.

## Questions to carry forward

- Do the language-match effects replicate with several songs and unfamiliar lyrics?
- Does a within-person design reproduce the large reported differences?
- Which traits distinguish adaptation from self-selection among habitual listeners?
- What happens when motivation, persistence, and comprehension are measured together?
